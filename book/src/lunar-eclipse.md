# A lunar eclipse

A solar eclipse falls on a strip of the Earth. A lunar eclipse looks the same from everywhere
the Moon is up, so its map answers a different question: who can see it.

## The figure

The inset is the figure from the *Five Millennium Canon of Lunar Eclipses*, with north up and
east to the left, as the sky is seen. The two discs are the Earth's penumbra and umbra at the
Moon's distance. The Moon is drawn at each contact and at greatest eclipse, the line through
them is its path, and the dashed line is the ecliptic. A further disc follows the time bar.

## The globe

The shading shows how much of the eclipse a place can see. Where it is clear, the Moon is up
for all of it. Where it is darkest, none of it. The magenta great circle is the horizon of the
moment, and the cross is the point with the Moon overhead.

## The numbers

There are two magnitudes. The **penumbral magnitude** is the fraction of the Moon's diameter
inside the penumbra at greatest eclipse, and the **umbral magnitude** is the same for the
umbra. An umbral magnitude above 1 means the whole Moon is in the umbra, and the eclipse is
total.

**Gamma** is the Moon's closest distance from the shadow's axis, in Earth equatorial radii.

The eclipse of 2029 June 26 has gamma 0.0124, so the Moon passes almost through the middle of
the shadow. Its umbral magnitude is 1.8436, and totality lasts 101.9 minutes within 219.5 of
partial phases and 335.1 of penumbral ones.

The contacts are named P1 to P4 for the penumbra and U1 to U4 for the umbra. P1 and P4 are the
first and last touch of the penumbra, U1 and U4 of the umbra, and U2 and U3 bound totality.

## The shadow is larger than geometry says

Sunlight passing through the Earth's atmosphere makes the shadow slightly larger than the solid
Earth would cast. The Canon follows Danjon in widening the shadow by enlarging the Earth's
radius, and so does this project, with a factor of 1.01.
