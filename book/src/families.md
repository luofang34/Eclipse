# Families of eclipses

Eclipses repeat. The NASA catalogues number them so that the repetition can be read off.

## Finding them

A solar eclipse can only happen at a new moon and a lunar eclipse only at a full moon. So
`eclipse-core` needs no search through time. For each lunation of a year it asks whether the
solar and lunar candidates of that lunation are eclipses at all, which is why opening a year
takes about a second in the browser.

Lunation 0 is the new moon of 2000 January 6, as the catalogues count, and the mean synodic
month is 29.530588861 days.

## The Saros

Two eclipses 223 lunations apart are nearly the same eclipse. That is a Saros: 18 years and 10
or 11 days, with the Sun, the Moon and the Moon's node back in almost the same arrangement. The
extra third of a day means the Earth has turned a third further, so each eclipse of a series
falls about 120° west of the last.

The total eclipse of 1919 May 29, at which relativity's bending of light was measured, and the
one of 2027 August 2 are six Saros apart, both in series 136:

Compare it with [the 2027 eclipse](../?date=2027-08-02). They are the same family: lunations
-997 and 341, 1,338 apart. Each eclipse of a series differs slightly from the one before, and
over the 108 years gamma has moved from -0.2955 to 0.1421 while the longest totality has
shortened from 6m51s to 6m23s.

## The inex

Two eclipses 358 lunations apart, an inex, lie in neighbouring series, the later one numbered
one lower. Together the Saros and the inex can number every eclipse: each one belongs to one
series, and the series in progress drift by one about every 383 lunations.

The two anchors are one catalogued eclipse of each kind. The solar eclipse of 2017 August 21,
lunation 218, is in series 145, and the lunar eclipse of 2026 March 3, lunation 323, is in
series 133. The rest follow, and the numbers agree with the Canons' for every eclipse.
